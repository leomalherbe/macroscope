In one paragraph

Seven countries share one currency and one central bank; the rest of the world is a given (its demand for the union's exports, its price level). Each country is the chapter-6 open economy of class 5, with firms that invest on bank credit added: households spend out of income and wealth, governments spend, tax and borrow, imports leak in proportion to output. Inside the union there is no exchange rate; with the rest of the world the rate is fixed, so the central bank's foreign reserves absorb whatever the union's current account does not balance. Unemployment and inflation are read on the model (Okun, Phillips) but do not feed back into it. The reference state is stationary: with no decisions and no shocks, every variable stays where it is, and every result you see is a deviation from that path.

Notation: X₋₁ is last year's value, X* the reference (stationary) value. Everything is real except trade with the rest of the world, where the import price in union currency is the exchange rate xr. Country subscripts are dropped when no confusion is possible.

Your levers and the administrator's shocks

A lever is a relative variation, in %, of the reference level, within ±10:

G = G*·(1 + pubdem/100)public demand (goods and services)
TR = TR*·(1 + benefits/100)transfers to households
θ = θ*·(1 + inctax/100)income-tax rate (θ* = 0.331)
τ = τ*·(1 + vat/100) ; τc = τ/(1+τ)consumption-tax rate (τ* = 0.10); τc = share of spending taken by the tax

The administrator's shocks of the year, the same for every member:

worlddemand of the rest of the world for the union's exports, %
ratecentral bank rate, in points (plus the voted changes)
confidencepropensity to consume out of income, %
importspropensity to import, %
devaljump of the exchange rate xr, % (voted or forced devaluations add to it)
Public demand +10 is 10 % more public spending, i.e. about +2 points of GDP (G* = 0.20 Y*). Benefits +10 is +1.5 points of GDP (TR* = 0.15 Y*). Income tax +10 lifts the rate from 33.1 % to 36.4 %.

Households

YD = YF + r₋₁·(Bh₋₁ + M₋₁) + TR − T_incdisposable income
T_inc = θ·(YF + r₋₁·(Bh₋₁ + M₋₁))income tax
CE = α1·(1 + confidence/100)·YD + α2·V₋₁consumption spending (including the consumption tax)
C = (1 − τc)·CEconsumption of goods
V = V₋₁ + YD − CEwealth
Bh = V·min(λcap, λ0 + λ1·r)government bills held
M = (V − Bh)·ν ; H = (V − Bh)·(1 − ν)bank deposits ; cash
The restoring force. Households aim at a wealth target: when income rises they save part of it, wealth grows, consumption out of wealth catches up, until YD = CE again. That is why a one-off stimulus fades and a permanent one settles at a new level. A higher rate makes them hold more bills (and earn more interest) — a mildly expansionary side of the rate.

Firms and banks

I = I*·max(0.5, 1 − ι·(r₋₁ − r*))investment, reacts to last year's rate
AF = δ·K*amortisation funds retained by firms
K = K₋₁ + I − AF ; L = L₋₁ + I − AFcapital ; bank loans (L = K)
YF = Y − AF − r₋₁·L₋₁income distributed to households
Bb = max(0, M − L) ; A = max(0, L − M)banks: surplus deposits placed in bills / advances from the central bank
Horizontalist banks. Firms borrow what they need to invest; banks lend it and refinance at the central bank if deposits fall short. The rate acts on investment the following year, with ι = 12: +1 point on the rate cuts investment by 12 %, about −1.2 % of GDP. This is the contractionary channel of the rate — stronger than the interest-income channel above.

Government and the common central bank

T = T_inc + τc·CEtotal taxes
Fcb = r₋₁·(Bcb₋₁ + A₋₁)central-bank profit, rebated to the government
B = B₋₁ + G + TR + r₋₁·B₋₁ − T − Fcbgovernment debt (bills)
Bcb = B − Bh − Bbthe central bank buys what nobody else holds
PB = T + Fcb − G − TR − r₋₁·B₋₁public balance (shown in points of GDP)
No market discipline inside the union: the central bank absorbs the bills households and banks do not want, at the common rate. The budget cost of a rate rise comes the following year (debt service on B₋₁ at last year's rate; public debt is about 105 % of GDP at the reference). Public debt ratios are reported, not constrained.

Trade: inside the union and with the rest of the world

IM = μ·(1 + imports/100)·Yimports in volume
IMU = (1 − sR)·IMfrom the union, split among partners j in proportion to their size
IMR = sR·IM·(xr₋₁/xr0)^(−ε)from the rest of the world (price effect with a one-year lag)
IMV = IMU + xr·IMRvalue of imports in union currency
X = Σ_j s_ji·IMU_j + XRexports = partners' imports from me + exports to the rest of the world
XR = xR*·(1 + world/100)·(xr/xr0)^ηexports to the rest of the world
Y = C + G + I + X − IMVoutput, solved simultaneously for the seven members
TB = X − IMVtrade balance (shown in points of GDP)
Leakages. μ = 0.30: thirty cents of every euro of demand go to imports, half of them to union partners (your stimulus is their export) and half to the rest of the world (lost for the union's reserves). After a devaluation the import bill rises first (prices move at once, volumes with a year's lag — the J-curve), then exports (η = 0.7) and imports (ε = 0.7) adjust: 0.7 + 0.7 > 1, the Marshall–Lerner condition holds.

The union and its reserves

CA = Σ_i (XR_i − xr·IMR_i)current account of the union with the rest of the world
R = R₋₁ + CA/xrforeign reserves, in world currency
xr = xr₋₁·(1 + deval/100)fixed rate: moves only by a (voted or forced) devaluation
NWcb = NWcb₋₁ + (xr − xr₋₁)·R₋₁the central bank's valuation gain on a devaluation
Σ_i H_i = Σ_i (Bcb_i + A_i) + xr·R − NWcbthe central bank's balance sheet closes every year
R0 = 3 months of RoW imports ; alert if R < 0.25·R0initial stock and alert line
A shared resource. Reserves are the union's, not any member's. Everybody's current account drains or fills the same stock, and the devaluation that follows an exhaustion hits every member's import prices. Orders of magnitude: a world-demand shock of −10 % costs about 30 % of R₀ per year if nobody reacts.

Unemployment and inflation — two thermometers

gap = 100·(Y/Y* − 1)output gap, %
u = u* − β·gapunemployment rate (Okun), u* = 7, β = 0.4
π = π* + κ·gap + ρ·Δτ·100 + ω·100·(xr − xr₋₁)/xr₋₁inflation (Phillips, plus tax and import-price pass-through), π* = 2
Read on the model, not fed back: prices do not change anybody's behaviour inside the union. A consumption-tax rise is passed into prices once (ρ = 0.6); a devaluation of 10 % adds 1.5 points of inflation (ω = 0.15) the year it happens. GDP +1 % buys −0.4 point of unemployment and costs +0.3 point of inflation.

The rules around the model: votes and the reserves crisis

Rate voteWith its decisions of the year, each country votes cut, hold or hike; one country, one vote. A majority of the playing countries moves the rate by 0.5 point, permanently (the changes add up over the years), on top of the administrator's rate shock; bounded between 0 and 10 %. Secret while the round is open.
AlertChecked at the end of each year: reserves below 25 % of the initial stock.
Crisis voteWhen the union ends a year under alert, the next year opens with a vote, before any decision: austerity (every member must keep public demand and benefits at least 5 % below the reference that year) or devaluation (10 %). Devaluation needs a strict majority of the playing countries; a country that does not vote counts as austerity. The result is announced, then decisions open.
Forced devaluationReserves at or below zero and a current-account deficit at the end of the previous year: a devaluation of 15 % is applied, without a vote. Once the current account is back in surplus, the union is repaying its foreign borrowing and the ordinary crisis vote applies again.
ObjectiveR = 0.2·ΔGDP − 0.2·Δu − 0.2·Δπ + 0.2·ΔPB + 0.2·ΔTB: the score of the year (deviations from the reference: GDP in %, the rest in points). The ranking uses the cumulative R, the sum of the yearly scores.
